---
title: Glottodemiurgy
ref: glotodemiurgia
---

It is in human nature to be a medium for language. We may be disposed to understand language as the house of being, or even to entertain the idea that we are predisposed to linguistic exercise; the human will to power lies in performing language, being the formation of the content that fills space. We are embodied by language throughout our entire existence, and we suffer from the eternal superficial dialectic that dictates human nature: the dialectic of form — performance and interpretation —, the content that uses us remaining, in fact, inaccessible. Man is the interpreter of language, and form, the interpreter of content.

The Dialectic of Form takes place as a Locomotion by Alternating Pivoting. First, we have Interpretation (*interpretatio*; to be a medium), which leads to the act of interpreting — which is nothing more than being a medium for language. Next, we have Performance (*parfourmer*; to bring to term), which concerns man giving form to language with the purpose of being interpreted. The result of this movement is maculation: the interpretation of performance, which leads us to a desperate attempt to carry truth across — language interpreted as it was —, yet the most one can do is interpret the performance. For instance, pain is born of inaccessible linguistic truth and must therefore be interpreted; once interpreted by the one who feels it, he attempts to carry the pain across through performance. In the end, the dialectical movement of Interpretation and Performance leads us to maculation — the interpretive attribution of individual pain —, and this is the engine of language.

Man does not use words from within to express what he feels; he borrows the language that exists outside him in order to perform the sensible, so that someone may interpret his performance. Therefore, not even the sensible is innate to the individual; his own interpretation of the sensible is linguistic and superficial, just as his performance also makes use of language. Returning to the example of pain: it does not exist in the individual; it is an interpreted sensation, though a justified one; when the individual needs to carry pain across — to a doctor, for example —, he is not seeking to perform pain itself, he will perform the sensible reception of pain, he will perform language itself. In the end, what happens is that, behind the whole dialectic of form, what there truly is, is language communicating with language itself and using man as a charismatic medium, man as the interpreter of content. Starting from this ontological principle, we glimpse a new demiurge and a new demiurgy: glottodemiurgy — in which all roads lead to language.

To exist is to perform, and not to exist is not to interpret. The person who has not yet been born exists; the person who has already died still exists. All are on the line of continuity, filling the space of language and forming material content: in plants, in beings, in the air. Therefore, as long as one can interpret the wind on the surface of one’s skin, Gorgias is wrong and everything exists.

Both performance and interpretation, since they do not exist within being, are superficial, given that they are machines in the social field that couple onto beings so that they may manifest themselves without ever reaching truth itself — a presupposed *noumenon*, for the artificer as much as for the maculator.

Performance is the superficial affirmation of the existence of being in the world of individuating subjection. To sing means to open one’s mouth so that higher forces may announce themselves. This holds artistically as well: sublime content cannot announce itself except through performance, and it is necessary to allow language to take being as the medium that wishes to perform. Be it a dance, a song, or a piece of acting, all performances are ecstatic manifestations of the individual and uncrossable sensible. Being embodies language, and its enraged performance is capable of humanity’s greatest feat: destroying the barrier of the discontinuity of individuals and bringing about continuity between beings. Therefore, rage leads to the performance of an ecstatic will — a performance of heightened *pathos*.

A fine illustration of how man uses ecstatic forms to perform lies in insufflation. When we purse our lips and blow air to inflate a soap bubble, for example, the wind leaving the mouth is not an inner air — it is not innate to the human being —; the air exists outside the body, and nothing exists within it. It is precisely in the process of inhaling an external force and casting it into our graceful manifestation that we understand: nothing is in the body or belongs to it; we are media of ecstatic forces, and we honor them.

Art is intrinsically about performance. We will never know how Chopin, Mozart, Bach, or Beethoven truly sound, since we have never heard them play. We can only attribute authorship to them, for they assembled the form of the work but did not perform it for our ears. Therefore, no interpretation of the Prelude, Op. 28, No. 4 will be the “original performance” — that is why it is called an interpretation — and we will never again hear Chopin play his own composition. It is precisely for this reason that performance is of the utmost importance: since there is no absolute authority of the author over his work, interpretation conditions performance to inevitable multiplicity.

As for Gorgias, who says, first, that nothing exists; then, that if something existed, we could not know it; and finally, that if we could know it, we could not explain it to anyone else, I must disagree and subvert his nihilism through tragedy: truth exists, we are discontinuous, and truth is inaccessible in its totality; that is why all we have left is language.
